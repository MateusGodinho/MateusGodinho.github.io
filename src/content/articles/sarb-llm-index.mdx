**Key takeaways**

- The LLM index is a meaningfully more sensitive instrument than the word-count dictionary:
  it tells hawkish holds from dovish ones, and cuts from hikes, where Henry's method reads
  almost everything as some shade of hawkish.
- Correlation with what the MPC actually did picks up sharply -- against the size of the
  rate move (bps), r goes from 0.48 to 0.80 for the combined index, 0.49 to 0.86 for
  statements alone.
- The method can tell whether a speech is actually about monetary policy stance at all --
  43% of the speech corpus (215 of 502) gets flagged off-topic and excluded, something a
  word dictionary has no way to know.
- It runs on the exact same historical corpus the dictionary method used -- no new
  documents, no new scraping, just a different way of reading what was already collected.
- The result is a more trustworthy live tracker of SARB communication going forward, plus a
  public reader anyone can paste a statement or speech into and get the same calibrated read.
- The bigger point sits underneath all of it: the gain here isn't an LLM doing research
  faster, it's an LLM doing the operational classification work itself, meeting after
  meeting, not just speeding up the person who used to do it by hand.

The [first piece](/articles/sarb-communication) in this series flagged its own ceiling: a
bag-of-words dictionary (Henry, 2008) can't read context, so it scores "growth" as hawkish
even in "growth has weakened sharply," and "risk" as dovish even in "the risk that inflation
runs too hot." The result was a hawkish/dovish index that tracked real policy directionally
but ran structurally hot -- even rate cuts averaged a positive (hawkish) score. That piece
named the fix as future work: an LLM-based classifier that reads for stance instead of
counting words, run alongside the dictionary as a check. This is that classifier, built and
tested against the same corpus.

## Why this isn't just "paste it into Claude"

Two design choices separate this from naively prompting a chatbot, and both come from
gaps the closest prior work names in itself.

**Whole documents, not isolated sentences.** [Kim, Spörer, Lee & Handschuh
(2024)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3677052.3698675) benchmarked GPT-4 and Llama-3-70B
against FinBERT and a word-count baseline (VADER) on FOMC minutes -- VADER managed 44.3%
accuracy against human-labeled sentences, general LLMs reached 68-79%. Their own stated
limitation: they classified sentences stripped of surrounding context, "an approach [that]
did not consider inter-sentence contexts." This project scores whole statements and
speeches, exactly the context their method had to discard.

**Structured output, not hopeful prompting.** That same paper spent real effort getting
GPT-4 to return parseable JSON -- one prompt design left 11.25% of replies unparseable,
and asking the model to explain its reasoning (chain-of-thought) made parsing *worse*, so
they dropped it. The Claude API's structured-output mode (`output_config` with a JSON
schema) makes this a non-issue: the response is validated server-side against a schema, so
the model can't return malformed output. That specific 2024 headache doesn't exist here.

The [IMF's 2025 large-scale study](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2025/06/06/from-text-to-quantified-insights-a-large-scale-llm-analysis-of-central-bank-communication-567522)
(Silva, Moriya & Veyrune -- 74,882 documents, 169 central banks, 1884-2025) is the scale
anchor for this whole approach, and it's worth being upfront about how much smaller this
project is by comparison: theirs is a fine-tuned model trained to classify stance,
sentiment, and audience separately, across the largest central-bank text corpus assembled
to date. This project isn't a different approach to that same problem so much as a
simplification of it -- one institution, one dimension, an explicit hawkish-dovish read of
SARB's own communication, produced with a general-purpose model calibrated by hand rather
than fine-tuned on a purpose-built dataset. What the two share is the diagnosis: the IMF
paper names, almost verbatim, the same failure mode this project set out to fix --
"risk-related statements could be misclassified as negative, while confidence-building
messages might be mistaken for positive sentiment." Context is what a bare word count is
missing, in both cases; the IMF paper is evidence that problem holds at scale, across the
whole universe of central-bank communication, not proof that this specific, narrower
implementation gets it right.

That points to the same conclusion this project keeps landing on: pasting a
statement into Claude and asking "is this hawkish or dovish?" would get you an answer, but
not a comparable one. What the IMF's categories and this project's calibration are both
standing in for is the same thing -- the specific communication history of one
institution. SARB's language has its own register, its own recurring phrases, its own
sense of what "elevated risk" or "a measured pace" means in context; a model reading cold,
with no anchors drawn from that history, has no way to know where a given statement sits
on this institution's own scale rather than some generic one. That's what the calibration
pass buys here: fifteen real documents and two adversarial sentences, checked by hand
against what SARB was actually signalling at each of those meetings, turned into anchors
the model reads every document against afterward. Skip that step and the model still
answers -- it just has nothing SARB-specific to compare the answer to.

## The method, briefly

Same corpus as before: 173 MPC statements, 502 English-language speeches, 1994-2026. Each
document is scored on the same -2 (most dovish) to +2 (most hawkish) scale, with anchors
given directly in the prompt rather than left to the model's own judgment, plus an
`on_topic` flag for documents that aren't really about policy stance at all -- a banknote
launch, a payments-system address, a green-finance speech, all of which the Henry
dictionary had scored as directional extremes purely on incidental vocabulary.

Calibration ran before anything else touched the full corpus: 15 real documents chosen to
span known extremes (the 2002 post-rand-crisis hawkish peak, the 2009/2019/2020 dovish
troughs, the July 2026 meeting this whole series exists because of) plus two hand-built
sentences designed to break a dictionary -- the exact "growth"/"risk" examples above --
run through both Claude Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 5, then reviewed by hand against what actually
happened at each of those meetings. Both models passed the two negation tests cleanly. The
one case where they disagreed with each other was July 2026 itself, and the smaller,
cheaper model's reading -- more measured, weighing the case for holding against the case
for hiking rather than leaning hard into the hawkish scenario -- held up better on review.
That settled the model choice: Haiku 4.5 for the full run, submitted as a single batch job
covering all 675 documents.

## Does it fix the skew?

Splitting all 173 meetings by what the MPC actually did:

| | hike | hold | cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Henry (dictionary) | +0.77 | +0.63 | +0.38 |
| LLM | **+1.01** | **+0.19** | **−0.98** |

The dictionary never goes negative -- even a cut reads hawkish on average, the calibration
problem the original piece could only document, not fix. The LLM index crosses zero in the
right direction for every bucket. Correlation with the actual size of the rate move (bps)
moves the same way: **r = 0.48 → 0.80** for the combined index, **r = 0.49 → 0.86** for
statements alone.

<iframe src="/charts/llm-contribution-full.html" title="SARB LLM index: contribution to the combined index, by meeting" style="width:100%; height:480px; border:none; border-radius:8px;" />

On the meeting that motivated this entire series: the Henry index read July 2026 at +0.78.
The LLM combined index reads it at **+0.53** -- still on the hawkish side of neutral, but
markedly more measured, which is a closer match to how that meeting's communication was
actually described at the time (hawkish on the external backdrop, benign on the domestic
outlook) than the dictionary's flatter, hotter read.

One more number worth naming: 215 of 502 speeches (43%) got flagged `on_topic: false` and
were excluded from the aggregation rather than counted as neutral. Spot-checking a random
sample of the excluded titles turns up exactly what you'd expect -- addresses on SWIFT,
payment-system launches, labour law conferences, university speeches -- confirming this
isn't the model being trigger-happy; it's a large share of the speech corpus genuinely not
being about policy stance, something the topic model in the first piece already showed
qualitatively.

That exclusion is also part of why the speech bars above look so much smaller under the
LLM than under Henry, though not the biggest part. Two effects are at work, and they're not
the same size. The on-topic filter cuts the average number of speeches counted per meeting
window from 2.37 (Henry, everything included) to 1.31 (LLM, off-topic ones dropped) -- a
real, mechanical reduction. But it doesn't explain the gap on its own: Henry's mean score
across every speech was +0.756, against +0.397 for the LLM across on-topic speeches only --
roughly half, even after the filter already removed the speeches with nothing to do with
policy. And those excluded speeches scored a mean of +0.036, near zero rather than extreme,
so dropping them should have pulled the remaining average *up*, if anything, not down. The
larger driver is the same calibration difference already visible on the statements side:
the LLM reads even clearly on-topic speeches more moderately than the dictionary did. The
filtering is real, but it's the smaller of the two effects.

## What this doesn't settle

This is one run, not an average of several -- LLM output isn't fully deterministic even at
low temperature, and a second run would likely shift individual scores by a few tenths
without changing the picture above. The Haiku-over-Sonnet call rests on a single
differentiating case in calibration, not a large sample; it's a reasonable bet, not a
proof. The `on_topic` flag was spot-checked on a sample of exclusions, not audited across
all 215. And the model itself is a moving target: pin the exact version if you rerun this,
because "the same prompt" stops being the same experiment when the model behind it changes.

Two more, neither a defect and neither likely to get fixed. This calibration is SARB's, not
a general-purpose one: the anchors, the examples, and the July 2026 tie-break were all
chosen by reading SARB's own communication style, so pointing this same prompt at the Fed,
the ECB, or any other central bank wouldn't transfer cleanly -- each would need its own
calibration pass against its own history first. And the corpus has a hard ceiling this
method can't see past: SARB officials give remarks at private events, closed-door
briefings, and calls that never become a public transcript. That material would very
plausibly sharpen what the model has learned about how these specific speakers actually
talk, but there's no path to recovering it -- it was never public to begin with.
