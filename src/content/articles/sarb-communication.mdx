**Key takeaways**

- Speech volume roughly tripled from the early 2000s to a 2017 peak, then nearly halved by
  2019 -- *before* COVID. MPC statements, by contrast, have been a metronome: six meetings
  a year, every year, since 1999.
- 2017 stacks up: peak speech volume, the pivot to an explicit 4.5% midpoint, the Financial
  Sector Regulation Act, and the ANC's nationalisation resolution -- three candidate
  explanations, none proven, none exclusive.
- The inflation target changed in *kind* three times in 26 years: adopted in 2000, pivoted
  to the 4.5% midpoint in 2017, replaced with a 3% point target in 2025.
- Topic modelling on speeches reads like a timeline: exchange rate policy dominated
  pre-2010, financial stability rose structurally after 2008, and inflation itself has
  been the largest single theme since the mid-2010s.
- A replicated Henry (2008) hawkish/dovish index tracks real policy directionally (hikes >
  holds > cuts) but runs hot throughout, speeches more so than statements. By that history,
  the "surprise" July 2026 hold wasn't much of one: similarly hawkish meetings have held
  52% of the time, and conditioned on the cycle position, it's closer to a coin flip.

## 1. Setting the stage

du Rand, Erasmus, Hollander, Reid and van Lill (2021) ran the definitive text-mining study
of SARB communication, covering everything the Bank said publicly from 1994 through 2020. Six
years on, the world it described has moved: a pandemic and its policy response, the
fastest hiking cycle of the inflation-targeting era, and -- as of November 2025 -- a
redefinition of the target itself. This piece updates that picture through July 2026, adds
a rate-decision-validated hawkish/dovish index that the original didn't build (Section 3),
and closes by turning that index on the meeting that motivated this whole exercise
(Section 4).

The corpus: every MPC statement on record (173, since the Committee's first meeting in
October 1999) and every speech by a Governor, Deputy Governor, or other senior official on
record on the SARB's own site (604, since August 1994; 511 were successfully scraped, and
502 of those are in English and used throughout the analysis below -- the rest are official
translations of the same speeches into isiXhosa, isiZulu, or Xitsonga). Both were scraped
directly from resbank.co.za; code and data are on
[GitHub](https://github.com/MateusGodinho/sarb-hawkish-dove), including a notebook that
reproduces every chart and number in this piece from scratch.

<img src="/images/communication-volume.png" alt="SARB communication volume by year: MPC statements vs. speeches, with Governor tenures and inflation-target changes marked" />

One honest dent in that line: the visible dip in speeches across 2002-2006 is mostly an
archival gap, not a real slowdown. The SARB's own site still lists a normal 10-16 speeches
a year for that stretch, but most of the linked pages or PDFs from exactly that window are
now dead or were never digitised -- of the 93 speeches (of 604 indexed) we couldn't recover
any text for, 54% fall in 2002-2006 alone, against a background failure rate of under 10%
everywhere else. Treat that stretch of the speeches line as a lower bound, not a measurement.

Four governors cover the sample: Chris Stals, Tito Mboweni, Gill Marcus, and Lesetja
Kganyago (in office since November 2014). Layer the volume of speeches on top of that
timeline and a clear story shows up.

Speeches climbed steadily through the 2000s and accelerated hard under Gill Marcus
(2009-2014). She was explicit about why: in a 2014 address on central bank communication,
Marcus pointed to a 2011 survey finding that **45% of South Africans didn't know the SARB
existed**, and argued that more engagement "reduced antagonism towards the Bank,
particularly when unpopular policies have to be implemented." Communication, in her
framing, had become a policy instrument in its own right -- not just a courtesy.

The volume kept climbing into 2017, then rolled over. By 2019 it had nearly halved from
the 2017 peak, and that decline started a full year before Covid gave every central bank
a reason to cancel its calendar. Three candidate explanations sit on the table for why
2017 was the turning point -- we don't think any one of them settles it alone.

**The mandate had just been explained.** du Rand et al. themselves point to an
institutional driver behind the speech volume and complexity climbing through this
period: the SARB's mandate formally expanded to include financial stability, announced in
October 2010 (in the thick of the Marcus era) and culminating, seven years later, in the
Financial Sector Regulation Act (FSR Act No. 9), signed into law on 21 August 2017. As the
authors put it, MPC statements "adjust in response to crises and relevant institutional
changes such as the expansion of the mandate to include financial stability... [but do so]
in a very prudent and predictable manner" -- it was the speeches, aimed at "more
specialized audiences at universities, policy and society conferences, and the SARB's
AGM," that did the heavier lifting of explaining what the new mandate actually meant.
On this reading, speech volume was elevated for as long as there was a seven-year
legislative process to narrate -- and once the FSR Act was signed, part of the job was
simply done.

**The message got a number.** A more speculative reading, ours rather than the paper's:
2017 is also when SARB communication got specific about what success would look like --
the explicit pivot to the 4.5% midpoint gave the Bank one number to repeat instead of a
range to describe, at the same moment (per du Rand et al.) that its speeches were already
reaching a wider audience than ever. A simpler, more repeatable message may need fewer
speeches to land it.

**The Bank was under political attack.** The timing also lines up with the SARB's most
serious political fight of the period: a June 2017 Public Protector report that tried
(and ultimately failed, overturned in court) to change the Bank's mandate, followed by a
December 2017 ANC conference resolution calling for the SARB to be nationalised -- a fight
Kganyago would later describe as "barbarians at the gate." Whether a more guarded,
consolidated communication posture during that fight was deliberate is not something we
can prove from the data alone.

What *is* unambiguous is the 2020-2021 collapse (in-person events simply stopped
happening), and the partial recovery since.

The target itself is the other axis worth tracking. It moved three times in ways that
matter beyond a simple number: the 2000 adoption of inflation targeting; a 2017 shift in
language ("the MPC would prefer expectations to be anchored closer to the mid-point of
the target range" -- the first time this appears verbatim in the corpus, becoming
standard phrasing by March 2018); and the November 2025 replacement of the 3-6% range
with a 3% ± 1 percentage point target, ending 25 years of range-targeting.

## 2. What are they talking about? Topic evolution

du Rand et al. also ran their speeches corpus through a topic model -- Latent Dirichlet
Allocation (LDA), an unsupervised algorithm that groups words into themes purely from how
often they co-occur, with no labels supplied in advance. Following Coco and Viegi (2020),
they settled on six topics, and only for speeches: when they ran the same algorithm on MPC
statements, the topics that came out were indistinguishable from one another (every one
dominated by "inflation," "growth," "rate," "price" -- unsurprising, since every statement
is structurally built to discuss the same handful of inflation drivers). We reproduced that
check before doing anything else, and found the same thing: statements don't separate into
distinct topics, speeches do.

**Replicating the paper's setup, then pushing past it.** As a first check, we ran the exact
configuration described in the paper -- six topics, no researcher input into which words
end up where -- and it reproduced their six themes almost word for word, including a niche
one (regional payment-system cooperation, driven by words like "SADC" and "settlement")
that we wouldn't have guessed existed going in. That gave us confidence the pipeline was
sound. However, our purpose here differs from the paper's in one respect: where du Rand et
al. were satisfied with six broad themes sufficient to support a historical narrative, we
were specifically interested in a more granular decomposition -- fine enough to separate,
for instance, the *design* of the inflation-targeting regime (the mandate, the framework,
the objective) from the *state* of inflation at any given time (this quarter's number, the
forecast, expectations), two things a six-topic model collapses into one. At k=6, that
distinction is simply not available; achieving it requires more topics than the paper
used.

So we pushed the model further than the paper did, on two dimensions:

| | du Rand et al. (2021) | This update |
|---|---|---|
| Topics (k) | 6 | 12 raw topics -> 6 selected final themes |
| Vocabulary | single words only | single words *and* two-word phrases ("inflation targeting," "target range") |
| Rare-term cutoff | terms in >=50 documents (footnote 25) | terms in >=40 documents |
| Sample window | speeches through ~2019 | speeches through 9 July 2026 |

The phrase-level change (bigrams, in the jargon) did most of the work: once the model can
treat "inflation targeting" as a single atomic term rather than two separate common words,
it stops needing to lump every inflation-adjacent sentence into one bucket. Reaching a
stable final configuration took some iteration. We first tried k=15, which did cleanly
split the *design* of the inflation-targeting regime (mandate, framework, objective) from
its *day-to-day reading* (forecast, expectations) -- but it was more granular than we
needed, and testing several random seeds at that resolution showed some splits (notably
Financial Stability versus a general "global crisis" theme) were not stable: which
documents landed where shifted from one seed to the next. Dropping to k=10 for a leaner
model made that instability worse, not better. k=12 turned out to be the sweet spot --
Financial Stability and Global Crisis, Debt & Fiscal Risk separate cleanly and consistently
at that resolution, and it surfaced a theme we didn't have at all before: explicit
discussion of the SARB's own institutional independence and legal mandate (words like
"Act," "independence," "central banks").

Even with all of that, one split never held up: the *design* of the inflation-targeting
regime kept bleeding back into its *day-to-day reading*, and vice versa, across every
configuration we tried. We tested it explicitly rather than assuming it -- comparing the
raw weight the word "inflation" itself carried in each candidate topic -- and confirmed the
overlap was real, not a labelling accident. Rather than force a distinction the data won't
reliably support, we merged the two into a single "Inflation: Targeting & Outlook" theme.
Two more raw topics turned out to be the same underlying "crisis" content viewed through
different historical lenses (1997-98 Asian/Russian-crisis vocabulary versus later
sovereign-debt/IMF vocabulary) and were likewise merged into "Global Crisis, Debt & Fiscal
Risk." The remaining four raw topics were dropped as noise or as redundant restatements of
themes we already had. That left six final themes.

This second chart replicates du Rand et al.'s Figure 7: for every year, what share of the
*average* speech's content the model assigns to each theme (a soft measure -- most speeches
touch several themes at once; this tracks the weighted mix, not a single "dominant" topic
per speech). Read across all six panels and a fairly coherent history of the SARB's
communication priorities falls out:

- **Exchange Rate Policy** dominates the early sample, peaking at 33.7% of the average
  speech's content in 1996 -- the year of the first post-apartheid currency crisis -- stays
  elevated through the second rand crisis of 2001 (16.2%), then fades to near-zero by the
  2010s as inflation targeting took over as the organising framework for policy.
- **Inflation: Targeting & Outlook** spikes to 54.5% in 2000, the year the regime was
  adopted, dips through the mid-2000s, then climbs on a fairly persistent uptrend from the
  mid-2010s onward -- 48.3% in 2016, 56.9% in 2021, 48.0% in 2023 -- consistent with a
  central bank talking more, not less, about inflation once the post-pandemic price shock
  and the mandate debate around it took hold.
- **Global Crisis, Debt & Fiscal Risk** is the closest thing to a crisis dial: negligible
  through the 1990s, it rises steadily from 2008 (9.5%) to a sample-period high in 2013
  (27.1%) -- the global financial crisis and the multi-year European sovereign-debt crisis
  that followed it -- before receding, with smaller subsequent bumps in 2020 (16.6%, Covid)
  and 2023 (12.3%).
- **Financial Stability** is a story of structural, not cyclical, change: negligible before
  2008, then a step up that persists -- 23.8% in 2014, 25.1% in 2017, and a sample-period
  high of 34.5% in 2025 -- consistent with the mandate becoming a standing part of the
  SARB's job description rather than a response to any one event. The 2025 peak specifically
  is also a composition effect worth naming: only 16 speeches were delivered that year --
  well below the 2017 count -- and five of them are unusually dense in financial-stability
  vocabulary. Four are Fundi Tshazibana's, Deputy Governor and CEO of the Prudential
  Authority, whose speaking mandate *is* bank supervision (AI and financial stability,
  climate risk, green finance, supervisory leadership); the fifth is Kganyago's own address
  on the global Basel III deregulation debate. With a small denominator, a handful of
  on-mandate speeches from the right speaker is enough to set a series high.
- **Growth, Development & Labour Market** and **Financial and Payment System** are the two
  quieter themes -- present throughout, occasionally spiking (payment systems at 20.7% in
  1998, growth at 31.1% in 2003 and 37.8% in 2004), but both trail off to near-nothing in
  the most recent years of the sample.

## 3. A hawkish/dovish index for SARB communication

du Rand et al. build their sentiment index on the method from Erasmus and Hollander (2020):
a bag-of-words dictionary approach from Henry (2008), originally built for earnings-call
transcripts. Every document is scored by counting words from two lists -- "hawkish"
(positive, in Henry's original finance framing) and "dovish" (negative) -- and combining the
counts into `2 x (H - D) / (H + D)`, bounded between -2 and +2. It's a blunt instrument
next to the topic model in Section 2 -- no context, no negation-handling, just word counts
against a fixed dictionary -- but it's the same blunt instrument the literature uses, which
is the point of replicating it rather than inventing something more sophisticated.

We built three views of the same underlying score: statements-only (173 meetings),
speeches-only (502 English-language speeches), and a combined series that pools both. The
combined series is the one worth dwelling on, because it isn't just an average. Following
the paper's own logic -- that speeches between MPC meetings are part of the same
communication stream as the statement that follows them -- we assign every speech to the
meeting-window it falls into (the period from the previous meeting, exclusive, through the
current one, inclusive) and compute the combined reading for each meeting as an exact,
weighted decomposition: `combined = contribution from the statement + contribution from
that window's speeches`. That decomposition is what makes the second chart below possible --
for any meeting, you can see whether a hawkish or dovish reading was driven by what the MPC
itself said, or by what the Governor and deputies said in the weeks before it.

<iframe src="/charts/combined-index-full.html" title="SARB combined hawkish/dovish index (Henry 2008), 1994-2026" style="width:100%; height:480px; border:none; border-radius:8px;" />

Two sanity checks before reading anything into the shape of this series. First, does it
move with actual policy? Splitting the 155 meetings with a recorded rate decision by
outcome, the mean combined-index reading is 0.78 for hikes, 0.64 for holds, and 0.39 for
cuts -- the ordering a hawkish/dovish index should have, even though every bucket sits on
the positive (hawkish) side of zero. That last point is a real calibration issue, not a
rounding error: du Rand et al.'s own version of this index runs even hotter than ours over
the overlapping period, so a persistent hawkish skew looks like a property of the Henry
dictionary applied to central-bank text in general, not a bug specific to this
implementation -- central bankers, it turns out, use a lot of words Henry's finance
dictionary scores as "positive" (*stability*, *confidence*, *sound*) regardless of the
actual policy stance.

Second, do the extremes match history? The most hawkish reading in the sample sits at
2002 -- four separate meetings that year score above 1.0, the highest string of readings
anywhere in the 27 years -- squarely in the aftermath of the 2001 rand collapse and the
double-digit CPI print that followed it. The most dovish readings are 5 February 2009
(-0.24, deep in the global financial crisis cutting cycle), 21 November 2019 (-0.26,
ahead of the growth-driven cuts of early 2020), and 21 May 2020 (-0.20, the pandemic
emergency cut). Every one of these hawkish and dovish extremes sits exactly where a reader
of Section 1's timeline would expect it to fall.

<iframe src="/charts/contribution-full.html" title="SARB combined index decomposed into statement vs. speech contribution, by meeting" style="width:100%; height:480px; border:none; border-radius:8px;" />

The decomposition makes the mechanics visible. Take the most recent meeting, 23 July 2026
(a hold, at 7.00%): the statement itself contributes a mild +0.12 to the combined reading of
+0.78 -- the speeches given since the previous meeting account for the other +0.66. In
periods with a busy speaking calendar, the combined index is doing more than smoothing the
statement series; it's letting the weeks of public commentary around a meeting outvote the
meeting's own text.

**This is a first version.** The Henry (2008) dictionary was built for a different genre of
text (equity-analyst earnings calls) applied here to a central bank's, and the hawkish skew
documented above is the clearest sign that a purely dictionary-based method has a ceiling.
The natural next iteration is a second scoring method -- an LLM-based classifier that reads
for stance rather than counting words -- run alongside this one as a check on where the two
agree and where they don't. That comparison is future work, not something we've built yet.

The sample runs through the SARB's 23 July 2026 MPC meeting (a hold, at 7.00%) and the most
recent speech on record, delivered 9 July 2026.

## 4. So, about that July hold

This piece exists because of one meeting. Going into the 23 July 2026 MPC decision, the
majority of market pricing was for a hike -- the backdrop was an energy price shock tied to
the US-Iran conflict, the kind of exogenous, supply-side inflation risk that central banks
typically lean hawkish against. The SARB held at 7.00% instead. We're not going to
relitigate whether it *should* have hiked -- that's a monetary-policy argument, not a
text-mining one. What the index built in Section 3 can actually do is narrower and more
useful: not judge whether the communication was hawkish enough to have justified a hike, but
check how compatible a *hold* is with a reading this hawkish, by the same yardstick used
throughout this piece.

The combined index for that meeting is +0.78 -- well above the sample average of roughly
+0.5, and squarely in hawkish territory by this measure. The question isn't whether that
reading was high enough to back the market's hike call; it's whether a hold is a decision
this method would recognise as normal at a reading that hawkish. So we pulled every meeting
since 1999 whose combined index lands within 0.05 of July's reading -- 21 meetings, spanning
five different SARB rate cycles -- and looked at what the Committee actually did each time:

Of these 21 comparable-communication meetings, 11 (52%) held, 6 (29%) cut, and only 4 (19%)
hiked. A hawkish-reading meeting has historically been roughly *twice as likely* to end in a
hold as a hike, and nearly as likely to end in a cut as a hike. On that base rate, the July
2026 hold isn't the surprise it was priced as -- it's the modal outcome for a meeting that
talks like this one did.

**But that base rate mixes meetings from very different points in the rate cycle**, and
July 2026 wasn't just hawkish-sounding -- it also came right after a hike (May 2026, to
7.00%), which is a meaningfully different starting point than a hawkish reading that follows
a hold or a cut. So we narrowed further: of the 21 comparables above, only five *also*
followed a hike, the same position in the cycle as July 2026:

That's a much closer call: 3 of 5 (60%) hiked again, 2 of 5 (40%) held -- nowhere near the
lopsided 52/29/19 split of the full comparison set. In other words, the unconditional base
rate's lean toward "hold" is being driven largely by hawkish-sounding meetings that came
after a hold or a cut, where a hold is obviously the path of least resistance. Once a hiking
cycle is actually underway, a similarly hawkish reading is closer to a coin flip between
"one more hike" and "pause here" -- which is exactly the honest answer to what this piece
set out to check: the index on its own doesn't clearly justify continuing the cycle, but it
doesn't clearly justify stopping it either. Whatever tipped July 2026 toward a hold, it
wasn't something this communication-only measure could have called in advance.

**Does splitting statements from speeches sharpen any of this?** The combined index pools
two very different kinds of document, and Section 2's topic model already hinted that
speeches range far more broadly than statements -- a Governor's speech might spend half its
content on financial stability, global growth, or the payment system before ever touching
the current inflation print, while an MPC statement exists for exactly one purpose: to
justify that meeting's rate decision. If speeches are structurally noisier on the specific
question this piece is asking, redoing the same ±0.05 exercise on each channel alone should
show it.

**Speeches alone.** Scored purely on the average tone of speeches in the window before each
meeting (excluding windows with none), 11 historical windows sit within 0.05 of July 2026's
speech-only reading (+0.77): 4 cut (36%), 4 held (36%), 3 hiked (27%) -- closer to a
three-way tie than any kind of usable signal. That lines up with a direct check we ran
separately: Henry-scored speeches correlate with the rate move at the meeting that follows
at only r=0.14 (Pearson, vs. the basis-point change). Speeches alone don't sharpen this
question, they blur it.

**Statements alone.** The MPC statement is the channel built specifically to justify a rate
decision, and scored on its own, July 2026's statement reads +0.83 -- hawkish even relative
to the combined index. Comparably-toned statements (13 within 0.05, 11 with a recorded
outcome) split 55% hold, 27% hike, 18% cut -- still hold-majority, but a shade closer to
even than the pooled comparison, and this channel correlates far better with the actual
decision (r=0.46) than speeches do.

| Channel | Comparable meetings (±0.05) | Hold | Hike | Cut | Pearson *r* vs. bps change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Combined (statements + speeches) | 21 | 52% | 19% | 29% | 0.47 |
| Statements alone | 13 (11 known) | 55% | 27% | 18% | 0.46 |
| Speeches alone (pre-meeting window) | 11 | 36% | 27% | 36% | 0.14 |

Even isolating the sharpest, most policy-relevant channel available, though, a hold is
still the plurality outcome for a similarly hawkish statement -- it doesn't flip the
verdict, it just narrows the margin slightly. No channel, alone or combined, makes a
decisive communication-based case that the SARB *should* have hiked in July 2026. The
statement is consistently the more informative signal of the two, both here and in the
direct correlation check; speeches, exactly as Section 2's topic model would predict, are
simply talking about too much else to reliably move the needle on this one question.

That's a superficial conclusion, deliberately -- even 21 meetings is a small sample, five
is smaller still, the real decision is driven by inflation and growth data this index
doesn't see, and communication tone plausibly *lags* rather than leads the decision (an MPC
that has already decided to hold may simply sound more hawkish to signal it isn't done
watching inflation). But that's exactly the lens this whole piece has been built around: not
what the SARB should do, but what its own words say about what it tends to do.

## Conclusion

Three takeaways, in decreasing order of confidence.

**Word-count methods are genuinely useful for reading what a central bank talks about, both
in volume and in substance.** The plain volume count (Section 1) and the topic model
(Section 2) each produced a clean, historically coherent narrative with no manual curation
beyond labelling the results -- exchange-rate crises, the post-2008 financial-stability
pivot, the inflation dominance of the last decade all fall out of the data on their own
terms. Nothing about the method is SARB-specific; it should travel to any central bank with
a comparable public record. The SARB is simply the one with an existing paper to replicate.

**Hawkish/dovish indices do correlate with actual policy, as expected -- but, at least for
the SARB, far more so for statements than for speeches.** The correlation with the rate
decision is real (statements: r=0.46; the combined index: r=0.47) and concentrated almost
entirely in the statement itself, the one document written specifically to justify that
decision. Speeches, scored on their own, correlate with the following meeting at only
r=0.14 -- present and directionally right, but too weak to lean on. Whether that gap is a
general feature of central-bank communication or particular to this bank, this dictionary,
or this sample is an open question a cross-bank version of this pipeline could answer; we
don't have that evidence yet.

**On the specific question this piece was built to answer, the index doesn't make the
case.** Communication reading this hawkish has historically preceded a hold as often as, or
more often than, a hike -- true of the combined index, and still true, if less starkly,
looking at the statement alone. The market's hike pricing ahead of 23 July 2026 was
reasonable given the geopolitical backdrop (the US-Iran energy shock) -- and it was almost
certainly shaped by other idiosyncratic pressures that week too, outside anything a
communication-only index can see -- but on the SARB's own words, hawkish tone included,
there was no clear signal pointing to a hike. If anything, the Bank's own
communication history was quietly consistent with what it actually did.

Sections 3 and 4 use the combined index, pooling statements and speeches into a single
communication stream per the paper's own logic. It's worth pulling the two channels apart
one more time to make the difference tangible, the same way Sections 1 and 2 kept treating
statements and speeches as two distinct communication habits rather than one.

The two series don't just look different -- they sit at different levels. Speeches average
+0.76 across the sample; statements average +0.50, a full quarter-point cooler. That's a
sizeable, systematic gap, not noise: the MPC statement is a terse, boilerplate-heavy
document written by committee to survive legal and market scrutiny, while a speech is one
official's take on a topic they chose, delivered to an audience that isn't parsing it for a
rate signal -- more room for the kind of confident, reassuring language the Henry
dictionary scores as hawkish. The statement series is also the tighter of the two, which is
exactly what you'd expect from 173 documents that all follow the same template, against
502 speeches that range from Governor's Reuters lectures to a Deputy Governor addressing a
regional payments conference. Read together with Section 3's finding that even the *pooled*
index runs hot, the takeaway is that speeches are doing most of that lifting -- if the goal
were the coolest, most policy-neutral read of SARB communication, statements alone would
get you closer to it than either the speeches series or the combined one. (Section 4 took
this split further, redoing the meeting-comparison exercise separately for each channel.)
